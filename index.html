01 / THE OPPORTUNITY AROUND US

More lunches.
More regular customers.

The opportunity: prepare Japanese meals for nearby workplaces and turn a first order into a weekly habit.

WHO COULD BUY?

Companies that serve lunch

Start with large workplaces that have a cafeteria or regularly order meals for their employees.

The first conversation is with the person who organizes lunch—or the company that runs their cafeteria.

WHAT DO WE OFFER?

A Japanese lunch every week

Offer a tasting, then try one paid lunch service. If employees enjoy it and the order is profitable, agree on a regular schedule.

WHAT ARE WE AIMING FOR?

3–6 regular customers

That is our proposed goal by the end of December. Start with a few customers we can serve well, then grow from there.

Where should we start?

Use the Saint-Patrick kitchen as our starting point.

Start nearbySaint-Henri / Sud-Ouest
Griffintown / Downtown / Old Montréal
Then go fartherSaint-Laurent / Dorval / West Island
For larger ordersLaval / North Shore
Longueuil / Brossard / South Shore

Proposed delivery areas. Test travel time at lunch hour before promising a delivery. Longer trips need enough meals to cover the extra cost.

How do we know a customer is worth pursuing?

  • Enough meals: how many people actually eat lunch there?
  • A regular need: could they order every week?
  • The right price: can we cover food, staff, packaging and delivery—and still make money?
  • A way in: who can approve a tasting and a new supplier?
  • A manageable delivery: can we arrive on time without disrupting restaurant service?

What could five regular customers bring in?

A simple example: each customer orders 150 meals, twice a week, at $13 per meal.

5regular customers×150meals per order×2orders per week×$13per meal$19,500in weekly sales

That would be $78,000 in sales over four weeks. Food, wages, packaging, delivery and other expenses still have to be paid from that amount.

An example, not a sales promise. The $13 price and order sizes must be tested with customers and the kitchen. The goal of 3–6 regular customers is also a working target.

Our first step: talk to the right companies.

We have a starting list of 50 companies and organizations to contact. We still need to confirm which have a cafeteria, how many meals they serve and whether they can buy from SHUSHU.

Those conversations will tell us how much business is within reach. The first goal is a few profitable, repeat orders we can reliably deliver.

02 / CUSTOMER FOCUS

Four segments.
One first priority.

Qualify the actual site and its buying process before spending on samples.

PRIORITY 01

Corporate cafeterias

Start with 300+ employees attached to a site, then verify attendance, existing food service, daily covers and willingness to trial a new vendor.

PRIORITY 02

Food-service operators

One operator can open several cafeterias. Identify who approves menus, supplier onboarding, margin and vendor rotation.

SECONDARY

Events & catering partners

Use team lunches and corporate events to generate contribution and introductions. Track event sales separately from recurring revenue.

2027+

Retail & supermarkets

Explore only after product consistency, packaging, shelf life and production economics are proven.

Who to contact

  • Workplace / Employee Experience: internal demand and sponsorship.
  • Facilities / Office Operations: access, service windows and logistics.
  • Food Services / Chef / Site Manager: menu and service fit.
  • Procurement: vendor approval and commercial terms.

Six qualifying questions

  • How many people eat on-site on each weekday?
  • Who runs the cafeteria and controls vendor selection?
  • Are outside food partners allowed under the current contract?
  • What is the vendor budget per meal and who pays?
  • What service format, allergens and delivery windows matter?
  • What must a pilot prove for a recurring program?

05 / MENU & OFFERS

Sushi first.
Keep it simple.

Start with the sushi the team already knows how to prepare efficiently. Add a few hot favourites, then explore other Asian dishes as customers ask for more.

OUR STARTING POINT

Sushi at the centre

A short selection of familiar rolls, prepared in batches and packed as lunch boxes or sharing platters. Use recipes and ingredients the kitchen already works with.

ALONGSIDE THE SUSHI

A few hot favourites

Offer one or two hot dishes for customers who want a warm lunch or a mixed order. Keep the choice small so preparation stays manageable.

LATER, IF THERE IS DEMAND

Other Asian options

Introduce additional dishes from the team’s existing Asian menus once regular orders are running smoothly. Let customer requests guide what comes next.

A simple first menu

MAIN OFFER / SUSHI

A small selection, easy to repeat

  • Sushi lunch box: a set assortment with a clear portion size and price.
  • Sushi sharing platter: for team lunches, meetings and events.
  • Vegetarian sushi box: a clearly labelled option within the same menu.

Choose a few rolls the team already prepares well, with shared ingredients and consistent portions. Confirm the exact selection with Lihn and the kitchen.

ADDITIONAL CHOICE / HOT FOOD

One or two dishes to start

For example, chicken teriyaki or karaage with rice, with gyoza as a possible side. Choose from dishes the team already makes efficiently.

Keep hot food and sushi in separate packaging and plan the delivery around both.

Suggested examples for kitchen review, not a final menu.

Three ways to sell it

REGULAR ORDERS

A sushi day at work

Start with one day a week: a set number of sushi boxes for a cafeteria or workplace. Add a hot option if there is enough demand.

FIRST ORDER

A tasting, then a lunch trial

Let the buyer taste the selection, then organize a paid lunch order with quantities agreed in advance. Ask what employees would order again.

MEETINGS & EVENTS

Platters for the team

Offer sushi platters with optional hot dishes for meetings, celebrations and company events. Agree portions, delivery and any serving needs before quoting.

Make the first orders easy to run

  • Keep the menu short and use ingredients already stocked by the kitchen.
  • Agree on an order deadline and minimum quantity before preparing food.
  • Check preparation time, ingredient costs, packaging and delivery costs for each format.
  • Confirm freshness, chilled storage and transport, allergen information and the handoff with the buyer.
  • After each trial, review what sold, what was left over and whether the customer wants a regular order.
The priority is sushi because it is familiar and practical for this team. Start with the simplest existing recipes, price them properly, and expand the menu only when orders justify it.

09 / CAPACITY FOLLOWS COMMITMENT

A promise the kitchen
can keep.

Validate the entire lunch service, from supplier order to the buyer’s handoff.

STAGE 1

Saint-Patrick kitchen

Measure unused production hours, equipment bottlenecks, storage and packing throughput. Preserve restaurant service quality.

STAGE 2

Dedicated production blocks

Create standard batch sheets, trained roles and fixed delivery windows once repeat orders justify them.

STAGE 3

Commissary decision

Compare contracted demand with demonstrated capacity, contribution and the full cost of additional space.

STAGE 4

Retail readiness

Treat shelf life, packaging, labeling and distribution as a separate development program for 2027+.

Before the first paid pilot

  • Recipe yields, portions and cost cards signed off.
  • Ingredient and allergen records; trained handoff owner.
  • Validated holding, transport and reheating process.
  • Timed loading, driving, unloading and serving test.
  • Packaging and delivery costs captured.
  • Order cutoff, cancellation terms and contingency plan agreed.

Capacity gate

Daily sellable meals = the lowest capacity across cooking, cooling/holding, packing and delivery.

Record actual meals per staff-hour and usable production hours. Plan a reserve for failures and restaurant peaks before promising volumes.

Expand only when: repeat demand is contracted, the existing bottleneck is measured, and incremental contribution supports the added fixed costs.
